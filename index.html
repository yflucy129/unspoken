A Visual Research Handbook  ·  ARS: Visual Culture

Unspoken

This handbook is the argument. Its form is the claim. The forms it holds are not illustrations of trauma — they are the shapes trauma makes when it is given a place to live.
Research question — How is childhood psychological trauma communicated through visual means, and what can images do that language cannot, particularly when the subject is too young to have words for what they experienced?

Once, before anything happened, there was a hand that did not yet know what a hand was. The hand reached out and touched a wall. The wall was warm.

This is how the world begins — by being touched.

The hand learned to hold a pencil. The pencil learned to make a mark. The mark learned to close itself into a shape.

Form 01
The Circle — the first form

Before a child has a word for what happened, they draw a circle.

Not because someone taught them. The circle is the first thing the hand knows how to do — close, and return. The head. The sun. The self.1

The therapist sitting across the table does not ask what it means. She watches instead how hard the pencil pressed into the paper. Whether the line trembled. Whether it closed all the way, or left a small opening at the bottom — a door the child did not know they were drawing.2

This is the brief no one writes down: how do you make form for something that arrived before language did?3

  1. Cathy A. Malchiodi notes that early circular forms typically emerge between ages two and three, often before recognizable figurative representation, and that clinicians treat these primary forms as developmental signposts rather than symbolic content. Malchiodi, Understanding Children's Drawings (New York: Guilford Press, 1998), 34–41.
  2. Lenore Terr argues that children traumatized before roughly age three rarely articulate the event verbally, instead reenacting it through behavior, posture, and the formal qualities of their visual production. Terr, Too Scared to Cry: Psychic Trauma in Childhood (New York: Basic Books, 1990), 184–202.
  3. W. J. T. Mitchell's "pictorial turn" frames this as a question for visual culture broadly: that images operate by a logic distinct from, not subordinate to, verbal language. Mitchell, What Do Pictures Want? The Lives and Loves of Images (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2005), xiii–xvii.
Form 02
The House — and the form began to extend itself

From the circle, walls began to grow. A floor. A roof that came to a point at the top, the way a child believes a roof should.

The house is what we draw next — after the self, the place that holds the self. The house means: I live somewhere. I am kept safe. Someone is inside with me.

But the study that followed seventeen thousand adults backwards through their lives found something it did not expect to find. The most common place a person is hurt is not outside. It is inside. The house is the structure that held it, and also the structure that kept anyone outside from seeing.4

The artist who made sculptures of houses her whole life — small houses balanced on top of each other, ready to fall — said: the lair is a place you go to be safe. The lair is also a trap.5

Same shape. Both things.6

  1. Vincent J. Felitti et al. surveyed 9,508 adult members of Kaiser Permanente in San Diego in the mid-1990s, finding that adverse childhood experiences — particularly those originating within the household — were dose-dependently associated with major adult health risks and causes of death. Felitti et al., "Relationship of Childhood Abuse and Household Dysfunction to Many of the Leading Causes of Death in Adults: The Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) Study," American Journal of Preventive Medicine 14, no. 4 (1998): 245–58.
  2. Bourgeois used the term "lair" repeatedly across her writings to describe domestic structures that contain both protection and threat. Louise Bourgeois, Destruction of the Father / Reconstruction of the Father: Writings and Interviews, 1923–1997, ed. Marie-Laure Bernadac and Hans-Ulrich Obrist (Cambridge: MIT Press, 1998), 144–46.
  3. Judith Herman situates this contradiction within a broader argument about the social structures that maintain silence around domestic violence and child abuse: trauma is held in place not only by individuals but by the institutions and intimacies that surround them. Herman, Trauma and Recovery: The Aftermath of Violence (New York: Basic Books, 1992), 7–32.
Form 03
The Body — and then something happened

Something happened inside the house. The story does not need to say what.

What matters is this: the child was too young to have a word for it. The part of the brain that turns experience into a story — that takes a thing that happened and makes it into a sentence — had not finished growing yet.7

So the experience went somewhere else. It went into the body.

It lives there now. In the muscles that flinch before the child knows why. In the breath that holds itself without being asked. In the way the hand draws a figure with arms that do not reach out, with a mouth that is a single closed line, with feet that do not quite touch the ground.8

The doctor who spent his life studying this wrote a sentence that became famous: the body keeps the score. He meant: the body remembers what the mind cannot.9

This is why a child's drawing of a person is never just a drawing of a person. It is a drawing of how it feels to live inside a body that knows something the child has not been told.10

  1. Kristen E. Buss, Jeffrey M. Warren, and Evette Horton review the developmental literature on early childhood trauma, noting that linguistic and narrative encoding of experience is not yet available to children during the period in which somatic and behavioral encoding predominates. Buss, Warren, and Horton, "Trauma and Treatment in Early Childhood: A Review of the Historical and Emerging Literature for Counselors," The Professional Counselor 5, no. 2 (2015): 225–37.
  2. Terr's longitudinal work on the Chowchilla kidnapping case provides extensive observation of how preverbal trauma surfaces through bodily reenactment, posture, and gestural drawing rather than through narrative report. Terr, Too Scared to Cry, 233–60.
  3. Bessel van der Kolk synthesizes three decades of neurobiological research to argue that traumatic memory is stored somatically — in the limbic system, the autonomic nervous system, and procedural motor pathways — rather than in the verbal-narrative regions of the cortex. Van der Kolk, The Body Keeps the Score: Brain, Mind, and Body in the Healing of Trauma (New York: Penguin Books, 2014), 88–110.
  4. On the formal reading of children's figurative drawings beyond iconographic content, see Malchiodi, Understanding Children's Drawings, 86–98, and the discussion of nonverbal expression in Lisa G. Eaton, David Doherty, and Renée Widrick, "A Review of Research and Methods Used to Establish Art Therapy as an Effective Treatment Method for Traumatized Children," The Arts in Psychotherapy 34, no. 3 (2007): 256–62.
Form 04
The Spiral — and the line began to repeat itself

The line that drew the body kept going. It did not stop where the body ended. It curled back. It went around again.

This is what suppression looks like when you draw it. Not a closed door — a line that returns. A thing the child cannot name, so the hand draws it instead, again, and again, and again, in smaller and smaller circles, until the center is just a point.

One philosopher wrote that emotions do not stay inside us. They circulate. They stick to things — to rooms, to objects, to the smell of certain weather. They accumulate. What we feel is partly what we feel, and partly what has been left on us by everyone who came before.11

The sculptor who made the same childhood into art for seventy years said: every day you have to abandon your past, or accept it. If you cannot accept it, you become a sculptor.12

She did not resolve it. She kept finding new shapes for it. That was the work.13

  1. Sara Ahmed's central argument in The Cultural Politics of Emotion is that affect is not an interior state but a circulating force that accumulates across surfaces, bodies, and objects, leaving residue over time. Ahmed, The Cultural Politics of Emotion, 2nd ed. (Edinburgh: Edinburgh University Press, 2014), 8–14.
  2. Bourgeois made this statement in a 1988 interview, later collected in her published writings. Bourgeois, Destruction of the Father / Reconstruction of the Father, 156.
  3. On Bourgeois's compulsive return to childhood material as artistic method rather than therapeutic resolution, see the introductory essays in Destruction of the Father / Reconstruction of the Father, 4–18; on the broader structure of repetition in trauma response, see Herman, Trauma and Recovery, 33–50.
Form 05
The Scatter — and then the form could not hold

At some point the line broke.

This is what trauma looks like when it is remembered, if it is remembered. Not a story with a beginning and an end. A scatter. Pieces arriving at odd angles. A smell. A color. A doorway. The feeling of a hand on the back of your neck. None of it in order.

The theorist of pictures wrote: images are not silent illustrations of what we already know how to say. Images think differently. They arrive sideways. They carry meaning the way bodies carry weight — without explanation.14

This is the answer, or part of an answer, to the question this project began with. What can images do that language cannot?

They can hold what does not fit into a sentence. They can show the shape of something without naming it. They can be a place where the unspeakable goes to live — not so that it disappears, but so that it has somewhere to be.15

A child who cannot say what happened draws a circle. The circle is not a confession. It is a beginning.

Colophon — A note on form

This handbook is not a diagnosis. It is not a catalog of what trauma looks like.

It is the brief — given to illustrators, designers, therapists, and anyone whose work is to find form for what does not have one yet.

The forms are not answers. They are questions, held in shape.
